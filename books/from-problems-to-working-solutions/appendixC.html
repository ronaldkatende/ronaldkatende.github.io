A Catalogue of Common Failure Modes

A useful handbook should tell readers how apparently successful projects go wrong. The following cases recur across disciplines. Each case gives a misleading conclusion and a better test.

The outcome is not the quantity of interest

Recorded medicine issues may represent available stock rather than actual demand. A traffic counter may miss vehicles when the sensor fails. A school attendance sheet may be filled only on days when the teacher is present. Ask what process produces the measured number and whether it changes under the proposed intervention.

The units are wrong

A water-volume balance may mix litres with litres per second, or a cost model may add annual and daily expenditure. List the units of every term and check dimensional consistency before numerical optimisation. Mathematically precise arithmetic with inconsistent units is not a model of the intended system.

A missing record becomes a zero

A blank stock count is treated as zero stock; an unvisited settlement is treated as zero demand; an absent survey answer is treated as no service use. Preserve missingness explicitly and investigate whether it depends on the outcome. Report bounds or scenarios when the quantity is not identifiable.

The test set leaks future information

A forecast for tomorrow uses a feature calculated from next week’s deliveries. A spatial prediction is tested by randomly withholding rows collected at the same monitoring station. Use a validation design that matches the proposed deployment decision, including time and site separation.

An optimiser solves the wrong problem

A facility-location solution minimises average distance but violates a maximum access requirement. A schedule satisfies a solver’s time budget but ignores travel or cleaning time. Revisit the problem record and write explicit feasibility checks with the affected service users.

A high score hides costly mistakes

A classifier is accurate overall but misses the rare cases that trigger serious harm. Evaluate errors in relation to operational consequences, calibration, uncertainty and affected groups. Compare the model with a simple baseline and permit abstention when essential information is missing.

Code tests pass but the operation fails

An offline application saves correctly in one session but duplicates a stock deduction when the same event is uploaded twice. A backup exists but cannot be restored by local staff. Test failure and recovery on intended devices and require independently verified reconciliation.

A pilot improves but the cause is unclear

Service improves after installation while new budgets, staff or supplier contracts change at the same time. Record the assignment mechanism and choose a defensible comparison. If a causal design is not available, report the change as an observation with explicit alternative explanations.

A tool has no owner after the project ends

A student or external developer leaves, maintenance stops and the only copy of the data becomes inaccessible. Specify ownership, documentation, export, training, spare parts, funding and a withdrawal procedure before scaling.

Success is copied into a different context

An algorithm that worked in one district is deployed elsewhere without checking data definitions, travel conditions, regulatory duties or service costs. Distinguish transferable mathematics from locally estimated inputs and intervention effects. Revalidate in the target environment.

A manuscript describes invented figures as field impact

Synthetic data are useful for explaining a method and checking a computation. They do not demonstrate real-world benefits. Label them clearly, use measured data only where authorised and available, and distinguish mathematical proofs, retrospective observations, operational tests and credible causal evidence.

The recurring remedy.

Start with the actual decision, ask what information and resources it requires, construct a checkable method, and make its limits visible. This is the common discipline joining all twelve chapters.
